# Prior art covers the parts, not the four-swipe whole

Every component of the proposed system has published precedent, but no source found in this review combines them. Researchers have already built four-group ambiguous keyboards decoded by language models (FlexType 2023, LowKeyEMG 2025) and studied LLM decoding of 2 to 5 keys (3-Key-Input 2026). Soft-posterior × LM fusion has been standard in BCIs and touch keyboards for two decades. Language has removed calibration in P300 spellers (Kindermans 2012) and in an acoustic keyboard attack (Zhuang et al. 2005), and LM pseudo-labels already recalibrate intracortical decoders (CORP 2023, B-CORP 2026). What is still open is the coupling. That means a low-cost 4-channel piezo band with only four in-air wrist swipes, a letter grouping optimized against the recognizer's measured confusion matrix rather than against text alone, decoding from soft per-stroke posteriors, and, most distinctive, using language after each rewear to pick among 24 cluster-to-direction permutations and then feed pseudo-labels into recalibration. That last step, cold-start symbol naming of a many-to-one wearable code, is the most defensible novelty claim. Even so, it has to be framed as an extension of the Kindermans/Zhuang lineage, not as a first. The main threats are concrete. FlexType already claims "first LM-aware grouping optimization". Kimura reports that mapping choice barely matters under a strong LM. Alignment to a previous wear could solve the permutation without any language. And the project's own results are single-participant, presegmented and same-session. The project figures used here (about 96% per-stroke accuracy after 4–8 calibration strokes, about 0.55 zero-calibration rewear macro-F1, about 93% post-rewear cluster purity) come from the project, not the literature, and this review did not re-verify them.

LowKeyEMG is the closest overall. It has the same four-letter-group interaction, a neural LM, and wrist/forearm sensing. Three of its design choices leave room for the proposal. The authors write that their word filter and character model "did not account for varying error distributions … instead assuming homoscedastic gesture errors". Their grouping uses Gumbel-softmax gradient descent on Brown-corpus word collisions, with no classifier confusion. And they collect **80 labeled events per gesture every day** ([LowKeyEMG](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19736v1)). Their own **online gesture error rates of 5.8–22.7% per participant** show how much an error-aware decoder could matter. That is their result, not evidence for this proposal. One open item matters for the paper's wording. The fetched text did not show whether LowKeyEMG passes softmax probabilities into its beam search, although one note records Levenshtein-tolerant character matching. Read the decoding section directly before writing "hard labels only".

## Four-group LM keyboards are solved for clean input; noisy input is the gap

The ambiguous-keyboard literature makes the 4-group idea itself unpatentable. MacKenzie's tabulation gives a 4-letter-key-plus-space design (TMK4) **KSPC 1.05 with disambiguation, conditional entropy 0.63 bits and an average candidate rank of 1.28**. A 2-letter-key design gives KSPC 1.55 ([MacKenzie, Ch. 5](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/chapter5.html)). Four-button and five-button predictive entry dates to Tanaka-Ishii et al. at COLING 2002 ([ACL C02-1149](https://preview.aclanthology.org/menus/C02-1149/)) and Dunlop's watch-top study in 2004 ([Strathprints 2554](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/2554/)). Grouping optimization by n-opt search over a character confusability matrix goes back to Lesher, Moulton and Higginbotham in 1998 ([PubMed 9865889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9865889/)). FlexType then added a long-span LM to that optimization. Its best unconstrained grouping reached **0.854 top-1 word accuracy offline versus 0.787 for alphabetical groups**, but users settled at similar speeds either way after about four hours ([FlexType](https://www.keithv.com/pub/flextype/gaines-flextype.pdf)).

The unambiguous alternative is a deterministic multi-stroke code. The four-direction codes MDITIM (KSPC 3.06, about 4.9 WPM), UDLR (KSPC 3.00, about 13.5 WPM) and 4-key EdgeWrite (15.95 WPM at session 10, 1.69% uncorrected errors) all spend about three strokes per letter and use no LM ([Wobbrock et al. CHI 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/chi-06.02.pdf)). H4-Writer's base-4 Huffman code reaches **KSPC 2.321 and 20.4 WPM by session 10** ([MacKenzie et al. UIST 2011](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/uist2011.html)). These codes are the strongest LM-free baseline for a four-symbol channel. By my own arithmetic, a stroke error rate of about 4% hits roughly 2.3–3 times more often per letter under these codes than under one stroke per letter.

The real gap is grouping optimized against the recognizer's own errors. The review found no paper that optimizes a fixed letter-to-gesture grouping jointly against a measured classifier confusion matrix and an LM decoder. FlexType's "badgram clarity" confusion matrix comes from LM mispredictions under exact taps. Lesher's and FingerText's matrices are corpus-derived ([FlexType](https://www.keithv.com/pub/flextype/gaines-flextype.pdf)). Noise-aware optimization does exist, but in neighbouring forms. Smith, Bi and Zhai optimized spatial gesture keyboards for "gesture clarity" and cut error rates by **37–52% versus Qwerty** ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/optimizing-touchscreen-keyboards-for-gesture-typing/)). Roark's Huffman scanning builds codes around the probability of selection errors (search-snippet claim, unverified in full text), and with an 8-gram LM it **nearly doubled scanning speed (24.5 vs 12.8 characters/min)** ([Roark et al. 2015](https://www.ohsu.edu/sites/default/files/2018-11/Huffman%20and%20Linear%20Scanning.pdf); [ACL W10-1304](https://aclanthology.org/W10-1304/)). An inference that has not been tested: the natural objective is expected top-k word accuracy under P(observed strokes | word) = ∏ confusion rows. FlexType's WER clarity is the noise-free special case of that objective.

Kimura is the counterpoint reviewers will raise. With error-free keys and GPT-4o, the choice of letter-to-key mapping moved CER by **less than 0.5 percentage points** (3 keys: 0.091 for sensible maps vs 0.096 worst case) ([3-Key-Input](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11642)). So the case for confusion-aware grouping has to rest on noisy input, where Kimura gives no evidence either way. Do not quote any 4-key number from that paper. It reports a significant 3→4 key improvement (dz = 0.57) but tabulates only 2, 3 and 5 keys.

## Wrist text entry splits between data-hungry sEMG and passive gesture bands

Meta's Nature paper is the benchmark for calibration-free wrist input. A generic model reaches **20.9 WPM handwriting closed-loop**, against 25.1 WPM for open-loop handwriting and about 36 WPM for a phone keyboard. Twenty minutes of personalization data improves offline CER by a median 16%. The decoder was **greedy CTC with no language model**, and the authors note that beam decoding with an LM "would have been possible" ([Meta, Nature 2025](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/42049/1/10.1038.s41586-025-09255-w.pdf)). That zero-calibration result rests on 48 dry electrode pins (16 bipolar channels at 2 kHz) and training data from up to 6,527 participants. The paper's four thumb swipes are navigation gestures, not letters. A commercial "Neural Handwriting" feature appeared in early access at CES 2026 ([Meta blog](https://www.meta.com/blog/ces-2026-meta-ray-ban-display-teleprompter-emg-handwriting-garmin-unified-cabin-university-of-utah-tetraski/)). Press reports from August 2026 describe a wider US rollout, and the sources disagree on whether it was still early access. No public WPM or accuracy figures exist for the product ([Ubergizmo](https://www.ubergizmo.com/2026/08/ray-ban-display-meta-rolls-out-neural-handwriting-for-silent-ai-interactions/); [UploadVR](https://www.uploadvr.com/neural-band-handwriting-recognition-start/)).

Among wrist-motion keyboards, WrisText is the closest directional analogue. It maps six wrist "whirl" directions to alphabetical letter keys with disambiguation and reaches **9.9 WPM on average and up to 15.2 WPM** ([WrisText](https://www.sfu.ca/~xingdong/papers/WrisText.pdf)). These figures come from a secondary source: the full text failed to load, so the sensing details and venue are unverified. Other wearables (TipText, BiTipText, TapType, FineType, RingGesture, and the recent LLM-decoded XR word-gesture work) use rings, cameras or rich trajectories rather than a handful of discrete symbols ([FlexType related work](https://www.keithv.com/pub/flextype/gaines-flextype.pdf); [TapType](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06001); [RingGesture](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18100); [FineType](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3714278)).

Piezoelectric wristbands are well established as gesture recognizers but have no text-entry record. Booth and Goldsmith's six-sensor piezo strap reports **97% mean accuracy on single-finger taps** ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40846-017-0303-8)). A Calgary thesis reports 93–98% (snippet-level; the thesis was not read) ([U Calgary](https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/items/5e34f101-7946-4afd-a9b3-96c6e38d994b)). WristFlex's FSR band exceeds 80% on pinches at 60.7 µW ([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/wristflex-low-power-gesture-input-with-wrist-worn-pressure-sensors/)). TapWristband (IEEE TMC 2025) turns wrist vibrations into a tap keypad ([IEEE CSDL](https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/tm/2025/04/10759301/221BudCLY3e)). All of these numbers are within-session. No piezo band with an explicit rewear protocol was found. The rewear evidence comes from other modalities. One FMG survey notes that doffing and donning "may cause severe performance degradation" ([PMC10708660](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10708660/)). An inter-day capacitive band with a triplet network averages **91.98%** ([IEEE TNSRE 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnsre.2022.3212705)). In EMG, rotation correction typically works in discrete 45° steps on 8-channel armbands, and arbitrary-angle correction needs dense arrays ([Sensors 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7070560/)). Myo-era practice recalibrates with one repetition per gesture ([J. Mobile Multimedia 2017](https://www.riverpublishers.com/journal/journal_articles/RP_Journal_1550-4646_1234.pdf)). The project's 4–8 strokes (1–2 per class) therefore sit at the low end of normal budgets, and its roughly 0.55 zero-calibration figure matches the qualitative "severe degradation" picture. Neither result is comparable to the multi-user numbers above.

Two cheap unsupervised baselines fill the space between zero calibration and labeled calibration. Riemannian re-centering needs only unlabeled data from the new session ([Zanini et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841546/)). Riemannian Procrustes adds stretching and rotation, which need some labels ([Rodrigues et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30596565/)). Covariance tangent-space classification is established for sEMG ([J. Neural Eng. 2024](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1741-2552/ad5107)). No application to piezo or FMG bands was found, which is an absence in a limited search, not a proven gap.

## Soft-posterior LM decoding is mature, but never on a four-symbol code

The noisy-channel recipe P(text | signal) ∝ P(signal | text) · P_LM(text) is old. For soft keyboards it cut errors by a factor of **1.67–1.87** ([Goodman et al. 2002](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/p194-goodman.pdf)). Sentence-level VelociTap decoding reached **41 WPM at 3% CER** on a watch-sized keyboard ([Vertanen et al. CHI 2015](https://pokristensson.com/pubs/VertanenEtAlCHI2015.pdf)). ERP spellers fused classifier evidence with n-gram priors by recursive Bayesian updating. Speier et al. report 40–60% bit-rate gains, and the RSVP Keyboard holds off committing to a string until it is confident ([PMC3360927](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3360927); [Orhan et al.](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/improved-accuracy-using-recursive-bayesian-estimation-based-langu/)). Intracortical BCIs converged on one pipeline: soft CTC phoneme logits, an n-gram WFST, then LLM rescoring. In the Brain-to-Text '24 benchmark, merging several decoders with a fine-tuned LLM cut WER from **9.7% to 5.8%** ([arXiv 2412.17227](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17227v1)). On the LLM side, a fine-tuned FLAN-T5 decodes noisy gestures and taps end to end at **93.1% and 95.4% top-1** ([Ma et al. CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3706598.3714314)). Methods now exist to get exact or approximate character-level distributions from subword LLMs, which removes tokenization bias when per-stroke evidence is multiplied in at character granularity ([Vieira et al. ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.03719); [Phan et al. ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09303)).

Each of these neighbours is missing one ingredient. 3-Key-Input has an LLM and a tiny alphabet but clean input. The BCI pipelines have soft posteriors and an LLM but a large, unambiguous alphabet. P300 fusion has soft evidence and an n-gram LM but no many-to-one letter code. The review found **no quantitative comparison of soft versus hard decoding for an ambiguous code**, so that is an open experiment, not a known result. Three estimates frame the stakes. Under hard decisions at 96% per stroke, a five-letter word has about a 0.96⁵ ≈ 82% chance that every stroke is correct. With errors spread uniformly, a 96%-accurate four-way channel carries about 1.7 bits per stroke. Kimura reports key-stream entropy of 1.54 bits/char at 3 keys and 2.26 at 5. If that measure is comparable, the 4-key stream sits close to the channel's capacity, so noise-free LLM results should not be expected to carry over. All three are estimates, not measured results.

The evidence also flags evaluation traps the project's rules already anticipate. Vocabulary size dominates headline accuracy. The same speech BCI reported **9.1% WER on 50 words versus 23.8% on 125,000 words** ([Willett et al. 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v620y2023i7976d10.1038_s41586-023-06377-x.html)). Domain matters too: GPT-4o ambiguous-key CER rose from 9.5% on business text to 20.1% on technical text ([3-Key-Input](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11642)). The project's protocol records one presegmented word at a time, with no sentence context. That is harder than the in-context settings of FlexType, LowKeyEMG and Kimura, so their numbers are upper references, not baselines to match. A four-swipe-only alphabet also lacks the space, undo and select gestures that FlexType and LowKeyEMG rely on, so continuous entry would need a delimiter design.

## Language-supervised calibration is old; naming permuted clusters after rewear is not

"Calibration-free via language" has a twenty-year pedigree, and the paper has to cite it. Zhuang, Zhou and Tygar clustered unlabeled keystroke sounds, labeled the clusters with an HMM over English plus spelling correction, and retrained a supervised classifier on the recovered text in a feedback loop. They recovered **up to 96% of characters** from 10 minutes of audio, with no labeled training recordings ([Zhuang et al.](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/%7Etygar/papers/Keyboard_Acoustic_Emanations_Revisited/TISSEC.pdf)). Structurally this is the proposal's cluster → name → pseudo-label loop, run in 2005 on a keyboard. Berger et al. reconstructed words from a single recording against a dictionary ([CCS 2006](https://www.avishaiwool.sites.tau.ac.il/_files/ugd/5dd4a3_2e14e058f2ef4c24ac188a26b1b5c6a4.pdf)). The decipherment literature supplies the algorithms. Ravi and Knight solve substitution ciphers exactly with a low-order character LM ([EMNLP 2008](https://aclanthology.org/D08-1085/)). With only 24 keys, exhaustive scoring is trivial. Wav2vec-U learns segment-to-phoneme mappings against unpaired text ([NeurIPS 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/ea159dc9788ffac311592613b7f71fbb-Abstract.html)).

In BCIs, Kindermans et al. trained P300 spellers fully unsupervised. Their trigram LM raised 3-epoch accuracy from **24.6% to 43.5%**, and their conclusion was that the LM "mainly helps to reduce the warm up period" ([NeurIPS 2012](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c042f4db68f23406c6cecf84a7ebb0fe-Paper.pdf)). Learning from label proportions later reached **84.5% correct online with no calibration (N = 13)** ([Hübner et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.07213)). The P300 unknown, however, is a binary target/non-target classifier whose symmetry the stimulus design breaks. It is not a permutation of symbol clusters. For continual recalibration, Jarosiewicz et al. retrained from inferred targets ([Sci. Transl. Med. 2015](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/virtual-typing-by-people-with-tetraplegia-using-a-self-calibratin)). CORP used LM-corrected output as pseudo-labels to hold **93.84% over 403 days** ([NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/83a14a36de4502bac5b580db36e81858-Abstract.html)). B-CORP replaced hard pseudo-labels with soft top-K marginalization and reports WER under 3% (abstract-level only) ([NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/151000)). All of these start from a decoder that was supervised on day 0.

Wearable EMG rewear adaptation is mature but uses no language. SCADANN combines domain-adversarial training with pseudo-labels that use temporal context, gaining **+8.6% location-to-location and +9.6% day-to-day** ([Côté-Allard et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11037)). Odeyemi and Zhang found that feature-statistic alignment was the only label-free method that helped every subject after electrode re-attachment, recovering roughly what one labeled repetition would ([arXiv 2607.27568](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27568)). Both assume class identities carry over from the source session. A rotated four-sensor ring breaks that assumption when the shift acts like a relabeling. Label-free manifold alignment (Degenhart 2020; NoMAD 2025) is the BCI analogue of "align to the last wear" ([CMU RI](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/stabilization-of-a-brain-computer-interface-via-the-alignment-of-low-dimensional-spaces-of-neural-activity); [Nat. Commun. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v16y2025i1d10.1038_s41467-025-59652-y.html)).

Four inferences follow, none tested. First, the global permutation is a discrete latent variable with 24 states that can be marginalized exactly on top of a Kindermans-style EM-with-LM model. Second, the right metric, following the warm-up lesson, is **words or strokes until the posterior over the 24 permutations concentrates**, not final accuracy alone. Third, identifiability depends on asymmetry. A grouping balanced for disambiguation flattens group frequencies and removes the unigram cue that separates permutations, so disambiguation-optimal and identification-optimal groupings may conflict. Fourth, the physical geometry might shrink the candidates to 8 dihedral relabelings. The project rules warn that cyclic reindexing is not physical sensor movement, so that has to be measured on real rewear data, not assumed.

## Synthesis: what is done, what is defensible, what threatens it

**Already done**, and to be cited rather than claimed: four-group ambiguous keyboards with LM disambiguation (Tanaka-Ishii 2002, Dunlop 2004, FlexType 2023, LowKeyEMG 2025), and LM-aware grouping optimization (Lesher 1998, FlexType). Also neural-LM and LLM decoding of very few keys (LowKeyEMG, 3-Key-Input), and soft-evidence × LM fusion (Goodman 2002, RSVP/P300, BCI CTC + LLM pipelines). Language-driven calibration-free decoding exists too (Zhuang 2005, Kindermans 2012, Hübner 2017), as do LM pseudo-label recalibration (CORP, B-CORP) and label-free EMG rewear adaptation (SCADANN, Odeyemi & Zhang). So does calibration-free wristband text entry at scale (Meta).

**Novel and defensible**, stated as "not found in our search" rather than "first": (1) cold-start resolution of a global cluster-to-direction permutation for a many-to-one wearable code after each rewear, by language, chained into soft pseudo-label recalibration; (2) grouping co-optimized against the recognizer's measured physical confusion matrix and an LM, as opposed to the text-only objectives of FlexType and LowKeyEMG; (3) measuring the gain of soft-posterior over hard-label decoding for an ambiguous 4-symbol code; (4) the platform, a low-cost passive 4-channel piezo band using in-air directional swipes, with explicit rewear evaluation, for which no prior text-entry study was found. The combination is the contribution. No single ingredient survives as a standalone first.

**Threats to novelty and to the claims.** The novelty threats are four. FlexType's "first LM-aware optimization" claim leaves only the recognizer-confusion angle. Kimura's under-0.5 pp mapping insensitivity could extend to noisy input if the LM dominates. Zhuang et al. already ran the full cluster-name-retrain loop. And alignment to a previous wear, or a dihedral restriction, could solve the permutation with no LM at all, which would demote language to a tie-breaker. Two further limits come from coverage: LowKeyEMG's soft-input status is unconfirmed, and the searches did not systematically scan CHI/UIST/IMWUT 2026 proceedings, Gaines's dissertation or WrisText's full text. On the evidence side, the project's results are single-participant, presegmented and same-session. A four-direction presegmented classifier does not establish continuous entry, WPM, cross-day or cross-person performance, or open-vocabulary recognition. The C170 test set has already been exposed, so confirmatory claims need a fresh, untouched holdout.

**Baselines reviewers will expect**:

| Component | Expected baselines |
|---|---|
| Grouping | Alphabetical (FlexType constrained), FlexType optimized, LowKeyEMG collision-optimized, frequency-balanced, random; same decoder and the same simulated confusion for every grouping |
| Code family | Unambiguous four-symbol codes (H4-Writer Huffman, 4-key EdgeWrite/MDITIM) at matched per-stroke error |
| Decoder | Hard argmax + dictionary; hard + LLM (Kimura-style); soft + n-gram/VelociTap-style beam; soft + n-gram + LLM rescoring; closed- vs open-vocabulary, with unseen word forms evaluated separately |
| Rewear calibration | Zero calibration; unlabeled re-centering / feature-statistic alignment; alignment to the previous wear; dihedral-restricted vs full 24-permutation search; Kindermans-style EM + LM; 4–8 labeled strokes as upper reference |
| Recalibration | No update vs hard pseudo-labels (CORP) vs soft top-K (B-CORP); a Zhuang-style feedback loop |
| Controls | Matched training-block label permutations, random-permutation and shuffled-posterior controls; per-class recall, prediction diversity, per-position and channel-map strata, seeds, test-access boundary |

**Recommended positioning.** Frame the paper as *language-supervised self-calibration for a low-bandwidth, low-cost wearable keyboard*. The central claim should be identifying the post-rewear symbol mapping and recalibrating from language alone, measured as strokes-to-identification and as accuracy recovered relative to labeled 4–8-stroke calibration. Treat confusion-aware grouping and soft-posterior decoding as supporting contributions, each tested by a clean ablation. Present Kindermans, Zhuang and CORP/B-CORP explicitly as the lineage being extended to a permutation-unknown, many-to-one wearable code. Present FlexType and LowKeyEMG as the interaction precedent. Present Meta as the high-cost alternative that buys calibration-freedom with data rather than language. Scope the empirical claims to the participant, hardware and session conditions actually tested.

## Conclusion

The literature changes where the contribution lies. The intuitive pitch, "four swipes plus an LLM", is already occupied by FlexType and LowKeyEMG. The fresh question is whether language can stand in for the user's labels in a regime where it has never been tested. That regime is extremely low per-symbol information (about 2 bits per stroke, by design ambiguous), a discrete global symmetry introduced by taking the band off and putting it back on, and only one word of context at a time. Both the ERP spellers and Zhuang's attack had either more context or more information per symbol, so neither guarantees success here, and a negative identifiability result would itself be publishable.

The quickest way to settle the open question is a simulation that needs no new recordings. Use the project's measured confusion matrices and cluster purity to estimate how many isolated words are needed to identify the correct one of 24 permutations, under each candidate grouping. That simulation decides whether the grouping should be tuned for disambiguation, for identifiability, or as a trade-off between them, before any user study is designed around it.
